The Genesis Sandbox — 1989

In 1989, as a new teacher discovering computing systems, I watched Bill Atkinson’s architecture unlock an entirely new network of portals. A stack of digital cards. Buttons you could see — and buttons you couldn’t.

Instead of wrestling with strict, unforgiving programming rules, an educator could script triggers in HyperTalk the same evening the vision arrived:

on mouseUp
  go to next card
  play "boing"
end mouseUp

• Intent first. Because the scripts read like plain English sentences, the educational vision mattered far more than the syntax.

• Students as producers. Learners stopped being passive viewers of information and became designers of it — building quizzes, portfolios, and branching stories.

• Free with every Macintosh. Apple bundled the tool free with every Macintosh, lowering the creative paywall and turning classrooms into sandboxes. But the sandbox was bound to grow.

Thirty-Seven Years of Tools

For thirty-seven years, as teachers and leaders, the workbench kept changing under our hands. Each new tool stripped away a little more of the mechanical tedium — acting like a digital steam engine designed to rescue us from the manual drudgery of data and layout. Each one asked less of us as operators and a little more of us as thinkers and creators. Each one lowered the floor.

FileMakerWe structured information so a school could finally see itself — building the data foundations that eventually made Power BI possible.
Power BIWe turned that data into a story a team could act on.
Macromedia & Multimedia CD-ROMsSound and pictures pressed onto a disc when “multimedia” was still a promise, teaching us the interactive design that led straight into Websites.
WebsitesWe traded the physical limits of plastic discs for the open architecture of the internet, reaching far past the walls of the building.
Vibe-coded appsI describe what I want, and the engine appears — including this HyperCard stack.

This year, I discovered vibe-coding and felt the floor drop straight down to language itself. It brings a game-changing question with a vengeance: as the engines take over, are we the horses being outpaced, or are we the ones directing the steam? The feeling is equal parts exhilarating, dizzying, and frightening. If this is the current pace, what will an ordinary day feel like for a teacher or a learner one year from now? Five years from now? I only wish I weren’t leaving just as it begins.

Project-Based Leadership Learning

The macro-evolution of the timeline is the story of a teacher learning to build. This card is about cultivating that same maker disposition across an entire school division.

We are born creators, yet institutional systems too often train it out of us. Project-based learning treats the learner, the teacher, and the leader as a maker, not a consumer. It is the same creative stance we discovered on the early workbench, scaled up to systemic leadership.

I have watched leaders learn this way. Hand them the space to build, and they don’t just solve isolated problems; they tend to an ecosystem. They stretch their thinking, pick up new skills, and deepen relational trust, all in the service of staff, students and families.

The capacity to create is a fundamental human trait. Learning is play. Play is the project. The joy is not a reward for the work; it is the work.

“Leadership is creating the conditions for creativity to surface and take root.”

Tending the Garden — 2026

In 1989, the tool sat on a floppy I held in my hand. I could open it, read it, break it, maybe fix it. When Apple walked away from HyperCard, my stacks still opened, for a while.

In 2026, I describe what I want and working software appears. This is a magical power. But at a cost. It runs in a space I rent and cannot see. When the vendor walks away, it simply stops.

Stack Kit — Colophon

This replica stack was built in June 2026 by describing it, in plain English, to an artificial intelligence. They call that vibe coding now. Mechanically, it feels identical to the act of 1989: type plain language, watch the software agree. The difference is technofeudal. We build now inside the digital fiefdoms of a handful of tech giants. In 1989, the compilation lived locally on a desktop. Today, the compute lives on someone else’s terms.

Specifications. 9 cards · 512 × 342 pixels · 1 bit · 50% dithered desktop · Chicago 12 · exactly one level of Undo.

Lineage. Modelled on the HyperCard Home stack (Apple, 1987–1993) and on annotated stacks like Mark Zimmermann’s Love’s Labour’s Lost (1996), preserved at the Internet Archive.

What travels. What travels across thirty-seven years is the stance. A teacher who discovers they can build does not unlearn that power. But as the floor of creation drops completely to language, we must read the lease carefully before we step onto it.

What does not. The model, the compute, the terms. Build freely. Read the lease.

To explore: open the Message Box (⌘M) and type go to card 3 · play flute · find garden · visual effect checkerboard. Or choose Objects ▸ New Button and double-click it. The scripts run.

For whoever finds this later. The garden is yours now. Pour la suite du monde.

The Messy Human Struggle

The steam engine does not replace the horse. As the thinkers at IDEAS remind us, it “imitates an abstraction of what a horse can do. It does not model its nature.” Artificial intelligence does the same with our minds. “It finds regularities in our strange human thought, to the point of imitating the chaotic way we communicate,” but real human intelligence lives entirely in the friction.

It lives in a classroom, watching a student “wrestle with language to try to articulate an idea that is new to them.” It lives in our willingness to be challenged by “the mess outside,” to disagree in good faith and refuse to settle into “the autopilot of our lives.” When everyone defers to the same models to prop up their learning, we risk losing the “pluralism that I think makes democracy work, that makes knowledge generate.”

To those who remain at the educational workbench: resist the autopilot. Protect the friction.

“Long live the struggle.”

Mamàhtawisiwin

The Wonder We Are Born With

Carried from Manitoba’s Indigenous education policy framework of the same name, this teaching reminds us of a foundational truth: we do not arrive in this world as empty vessels to be sorted and stamped. We are born intact, carrying an inherent gift meant to help people live a good life.

To place Mamàhtawisiwin at the centre of a classroom is a radical act. It is a direct refusal of the sorting factory.

In this space, progress isn’t measured by a letter grade, but by the awakening of purpose:

A person becomes an okihcite — a great heart — the moment they begin to share their gift. And one who goes above and beyond, actively connecting people to the very root of their existence, is e-mamahtawisit.

This teaching is not mine to own. I name its source to stay accountable to the framework’s vision and the Knowledge Carriers who protect it.

The Three Sisters

Corn, beans, and squash

In Braiding Sweetgrass, Robin Wall Kimmerer describes the Three Sisters: corn raises a scaffold, beans climb to fix nitrogen in the common soil, and squash spreads low to shade out weeds and hold moisture. They thrive not by competing, but by exchanging reciprocal gifts.

A classroom is no different. It is not a rigid monoculture, but a living polyculture.

Crucially, those roles are never fixed. A teacher and a learner can be all of these gifts to each other. In a single morning, a learner might shoot upward to raise a brilliant new scaffold of insight, while the teacher moves quietly to nourish the soil beneath them. We take turns growing, sheltering, and feeding. Here, difference is the nutrient, not the deficit.

We are all Treaty people

A Covenant, Not a Contract

On Treaty 1 territory, Treaty is not a historical footnote. It is a living covenant of relational accountability — lateral, reciprocal, and ongoing.

To lead through Treaty is to turn the soil of truth—confronting racism, dismantling colonialism, and facing the legacy of residential schools.

It is to widen the circle so that every student is met with the conditions they need to flourish.

It is to measure our success by the health of our relationships, recognizing that the community serves the child, not the mandate.

As long as the sun shines, the grass grows, and the river flows.

Bill Atkinson & John Sculley

Macworld Expo, August 1987

Bill Atkinson created HyperCard, and Apple CEO John Sculley unveiled it at the Macworld Expo. Then Apple did something radical: they bundled it free with every new Macintosh. That is almost certainly when it began flooding into schools.

Strikingly, HyperCard’s linking concepts went on to inspire early web browsers and the explosion of the World Wide Web — the stack of cards became the stack of pages.

HyperTalk

Scripting in plain English

HyperTalk was brilliant because it read almost like ordinary sentences: go to next card, play "boing", on mouseUp.

In a classroom, it let teachers and students build interactive quizzes, digital portfolios, and multimedia presentations long before PowerPoint was the standard or HTML was a household word.

The Message Box at the bottom of this stack (⌘M) speaks a small dialect of it. So do the buttons you can make in the Objects menu. Try it.

HyperStudio

The multimedia classroom sandbox

While HyperCard stayed famously black-and-white, HyperStudio carried the very same idea into full colour. On the iMac it ran with vivid graphics, sound, and scanned art that blew kids’ minds.

Crucially, it kept the exact same metaphor: you built cards and linked them with invisible buttons. The grammar transferred; the canvas grew.

1989 ↔ 2026

Parallel worlds

Intent-first creation. In 1989, HyperTalk let you type show button without touching binary. Today you describe the vision of an app in plain English (or French) and the AI handles the heavy lifting.

Democratizing the tech. Apple bundled HyperCard to turn everyday users — educators like me, and students — into creators, not consumers. Vibe coding does the same: it tears down the programming barrier and hands the keys to the thinkers, the creators, the teachers, the students.

The privilege of access. But the keys only reach those already in the room. HyperCard empowered the students who had a Macintosh in front of them; not every classroom did. The same caveat holds today. I have been fortunate in that access. Much of public education has not.

The Tending Philosophy

What the tools can and cannot carry

Focus on the craft. The builder gets to focus entirely on how the tool serves the person using it, leaving the plumbing to the system.

But tools change. The true structural anchor remains relationships, responsibility, shared honesty, judgment, and wisdom. The apps are not the garden.
